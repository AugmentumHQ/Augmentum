# Methodology for a Mixed-Methods Study of Remote Work Adoption and Organizational Outcomes

**Manuscript Reference**: WP-2025-0047-R2
**Prepared for**: *Journal of Organizational Behavior and Technology*
**Submission Date**: November 3, 2025

---

## 1. Introduction

This document describes the methodological framework for a multi-phase empirical investigation examining the antecedents and consequences of remote work adoption among knowledge workers in mid-to-large organizations (500+ employees) in the United States. The broader study, titled *Distributed Labor, Distributed Outcomes: A Mixed-Methods Examination of Remote Work and Firm-Level Productivity*, seeks to contribute to an emerging body of evidence navigating tensions between managerial preference for co-located work arrangements and documented heterogeneity in individual and organizational outcomes following the rapid normalization of remote and hybrid work environments during and after the 2020–2022 period.

**Primary Research Questions**:

1. To what extent does the degree of remote work adoption (measured as percentage of work hours conducted outside a formal office) predict employee-reported well-being, self-assessed productivity, and supervisory-assessed performance ratings?
2. Do organizational-level factors — specifically, organizational culture orientation (hierarchy versus clan, per the Competing Values Framework; Quinn & Rohrbaugh, 1983), leadership communication cadence, and technology provisioning adequacy — moderate the relationship between remote work intensity and the outcomes identified in RQ1?
3. What mechanisms do employees invoke to make sense of remote work arrangements, and how do these phenomenological accounts diverge across role hierarchy, geographic region, and industry sector?

The study employs a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018), in which a large-scale quantitative phase provides breadth and statistical generalizability, followed by a qualitative phase designed to contextualize and elaborate upon patterns identified in the quantitative data.

## 2. Research Design

### 2.1 Rationale for Mixed-Methods

A single-method approach — whether purely survey-based or exclusively interview-driven — would inadequately address the multidimensional nature of the research questions. Quantitative instruments excel at capturing variance across large populations and testing hypothesized relationships among latent constructs, but they do not illuminate the interpretive processes through which individuals assign meaning to their experiences. Conversely, qualitative inquiry yields dense, contextually rich accounts but lacks the statistical power to support generalized causal inferences (Teddlie & Tashakkori, 2009).

The sequential explanatory design was selected over concurrent or transformative alternatives because the qualitative phase is explicitly theory-elaborating: interview guide construction will be informed by quantitative findings, enabling targeted probing of unexpected statistical patterns (e.g., subgroup interactions that emerge from moderation analyses). This sequencing is consistent with a post-positivist ontological stance that acknowledges the social construction of workplace experience while retaining a commitment to systematic, reproducible inquiry.

### 2.2 Phases Overview

| Phase | Method | N | Objective |
|-------|--------|---|-----------|
| Phase 1 | Cross-sectional online survey | 2,847 | Test hypothesized relationships via structural equation modeling |
| Phase 2 | Semi-structured interviews | 42 | Elaborate statistical findings with phenomenological accounts |
| Phase 3 | Organizational document analysis | 18 orgs | Contextualize organizational moderators with archival policy data |

## 3. Sampling Strategy

### 3.1 Phase 1 (Survey) — Stratified Random Sampling

The target population for Phase 1 is full-time knowledge workers employed at organizations with 500 or more employees in the United States, who have been in their current role for a minimum of twelve months and who have performed at least some portion of their work remotely (defined as at least 10% of work hours per week) during the twelve months preceding data collection.

A stratified random sampling approach was employed to ensure representation across four a priori stratification dimensions:

1. **Industry sector**: Technology, Finance/Insurance, Healthcare, Education, Professional Services, Manufacturing (6 strata)
2. **Organizational size**: 500–1,999 employees, 2,000–9,999 employees, 10,000+ employees (3 strata)
3. **Remote work intensity**: Fully remote (≥90% remote), Hybrid (25–89% remote), Primarily on-site (10–24% remote) (3 strata)
4. **Geographic region**: Northeast, South, Midwest, West (4 strata)

This yields a 6×3×3×4 stratification matrix with 216 cells. Target cell sizes were computed using proportional allocation weighted by U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics workforce distribution data (BLS, 2024 Q2), with a minimum floor of 8 respondents per cell to ensure basic representativeness in underrepresented strata. The resulting target sample size was **N = 2,847**, with a design effect (DEFF) of 1.47 estimated from prior panel data, yielding an effective sample size of approximately N_eff = 1,936 for power calculations.

A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1; Faul et al., 2009) indicated that N_eff = 1,936 provides 0.99 statistical power to detect a small effect size (f² = 0.02) in multiple regression with up to 24 predictors at α = 0.05, and 0.95 power to detect interaction effects of f² = 0.005, consistent with typical moderation effect sizes in organizational behavior research (Aguinis et al., 2005).

Participants were recruited via a commercial survey panel provider (Qualtrics XM Panel) with quota enforcement to match stratification targets. Panel respondents were screened using three inclusion criteria embedded as gate questions at survey entry. Failed screener responses terminated the survey and were excluded from the analytical dataset.

### 3.2 Phase 2 (Interviews) — Purposive Maximum Variation Sampling

Phase 2 interview participants (N = 42) were recruited through purposive maximum variation sampling (Patton, 2015) from the Phase 1 survey respondents who indicated willingness to be re-contacted. Maximum variation sampling was selected to ensure the interview corpus spans the widest possible range of meaningful contrasts — specifically, contrasts across remote work intensity, industry sector, role hierarchy (individual contributor vs. people manager), and geographic region. A stratification matrix for interview sampling was derived from Phase 1 survey respondents' demographic data. Theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) guided termination decisions within each subgroup.

## 4. Data Collection

### 4.1 Survey Instrument Design

The Phase 1 survey instrument was developed through a multi-stage process involving item generation, expert review, and pilot testing.

**Outcome Constructs**:

- *Employee Well-Being* was operationalized using the 14-item WEMWBS (Warwick–Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale; Tennant et al., 2007), supplemented with 4 items adapted from Diener's SWLS (Satisfaction with Life Scale) to capture hedonic well-being.
- *Self-Assessed Productivity* was measured using a 7-item scale adapted from Nakrošienė et al. (2019), assessing focus, task completion, and collaboration effectiveness.
- *Supervisory Performance Ratings* were collected as single-item anchored numeric responses ("How would you characterize your most recent formal performance evaluation outcome?") with a 5-point response scale ranging from 1 (Significantly below expectations) to 5 (Significantly above expectations).

**Predictor and Moderator Constructs**:

- *Remote Work Intensity* was measured as a continuous proportion (0–100% of work hours per week remote, averaged over the prior 12 months).
- *Organizational Culture Orientation* used a shortened version of the CVF Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI; Cameron & Quinn, 2011), comprising 24 items across four culture archetypes.
- *Technology Provisioning Adequacy* used 6 items adapted from Venkatesh and colleagues' UTAUT2 (2012), focusing on facilitating conditions.
- *Leadership Communication Cadence* used 5 items measuring perceived frequency, clarity, and responsiveness of managerial communication via digital channels.

**Scale Format**: All multi-item scales used a 7-point Likert-type response format (1 = Strongly Disagree, 7 = Strongly Agree), chosen to maximize response differentiation while remaining within the range associated with psychometric optimality (Krosnick & Presser, 2010). Negatively worded items were included to detect acquiescence bias.

**Survey Administration**: The instrument was hosted on Qualtrics. Median anticipated completion time was 22 minutes based on pilot data. Participants received a $3.00 honorarium. All items were presented in randomized block order (construct-level randomization) to mitigate order effects, while preserving logical response flow within constructs.

### 4.2 Pilot Testing

Prior to full deployment, the instrument was administered to a convenience sample of N = 63 respondents drawn from professional networks of the research team. Pilot data were used to:

1. Estimate internal consistency reliabilities (Cronbach's alpha) and flag items with item-total correlations below 0.30;
2. Identify comprehension problems through cognitive think-aloud protocols (N = 8 participants);
3. Verify survey platform functionality, skip logic, and data export integrity; and
4. Refine estimated completion time.

Following pilot analysis, four items were reworded for clarity, one item was dropped due to low item-total correlation (r = 0.18), and one new item was added to the leadership communication scale based on recurring theme in think-aloud sessions.

### 4.3 Interview Protocol

Phase 2 interviews were conducted via video conferencing (Zoom) with audio/video recording after obtaining explicit informed consent. Each interview was semi-structured, guided by an interview protocol developed to probe themes anticipated from Phase 1 quantitative findings, while remaining flexible to emergent topics.

Interview duration ranged from 47 to 94 minutes (M = 68 minutes). Core protocol domains included: (a) daily experience narrative ("describe a typical remote workday from your perspective"), (b) perceived enablers and constraints, (c) social and collaborative dynamics, (d) career development concerns, and (e) manager and organizational context. Probing questions were calibrated based on each participant's Phase 1 survey responses.

## 5. Quantitative Analysis

### 5.1 Data Screening and Cleaning

Prior to primary analysis, data underwent screening for: (a) multivariate outliers via Mahalanobis distance (χ² critical value at p < .001); (b) insufficient effort responding, flagged by completion time below 8 minutes (< 2 SD below mean) or straight-lining across three or more Likert scales; (c) failed attention check items (two embedded items); and (d) item non-response above 20% per construct. Respondents failing any criterion were excluded. Final analytical sample: N = 2,714 (exclusion rate: 4.7%).

### 5.2 Measurement Model

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted in R (v4.3.1) using the *lavaan* package (Rosseel, 2012) to evaluate the measurement model for all reflective multi-item constructs. Model fit was evaluated using: CFI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06, and SRMR ≤ .08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Convergent validity was assessed via average variance extracted (AVE ≥ .50) and composite reliability (CR ≥ .70). Discriminant validity was assessed via the heterotrait–monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT < .85; Henseler et al., 2015).

### 5.3 Structural Equation Modeling

Hypothesized structural paths were estimated using a full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimator to address item-level missing data under a missing-at-random assumption. The structural model includes three endogenous outcome variables (well-being, self-assessed productivity, performance rating) and tests both direct effects of remote work intensity and moderated mediation paths involving organizational culture and technology provisioning as moderators.

**Effect Size Reporting**: Standardized path coefficients (β), along with 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (5,000 resamples), are reported for all structural paths. Effect sizes for moderation interactions are expressed as Cohen's *f*² (Cohen, 1988), with conventional benchmarks of *f*² = 0.02 (small), 0.15 (medium), and 0.35 (large).

### 5.4 Subgroup Comparisons

One-way ANOVA was used to compare mean outcomes across remote work intensity strata (fully remote vs. hybrid vs. primarily on-site). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons employed Tukey's HSD with family-wise error rate control. Effect size for ANOVA comparisons is reported as Cohen's *d* for pairwise differences and η² for overall group effects.

## 6. Qualitative Analysis

### 6.1 Transcription and Preparation

Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service, with identifying information pseudonymized at the transcription stage. Transcripts were reviewed by the interviewer for accuracy and uploaded to NVivo 14 for analysis.

### 6.2 Thematic Analysis

Interview data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2022), a theoretically flexible approach that allows themes to emerge inductively from data while remaining responsive to the theoretical sensitizing concepts established in the quantitative phase. Analysis proceeded through six iterative phases: familiarization, initial code generation, theme construction, review, definition and naming, and write-up.

Initial coding was conducted by two independent analysts (the primary investigator and a trained graduate research assistant), each coding a 20% overlap subset of transcripts. **Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa, yielding κ = 0.83**, indicating strong agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977). Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. Remaining transcripts were divided between analysts with spot-checks by the primary investigator.

### 6.3 Integration with Quantitative Findings

Qualitative themes were integrated with quantitative results using a "following a thread" joint display approach (Fetters et al., 2013). Participants' Phase 1 quantitative response profiles were used to select exemplary quotations that illuminate statistical patterns — for example, participants in the high remote work intensity/low organizational support quadrant were oversampled for quotations related to isolation and career visibility.

## 7. Validity and Reliability

**Internal Consistency**: Cronbach's alpha was computed for all multi-item scales in the pilot (N = 63) and full samples (N = 2,714). Acceptable reliability was defined as α ≥ .70. Final scale reliabilities ranged from α = .76 (performance rating context scale) to α = .94 (well-being composite), with a median of α = .84.

**Construct Validity**: Convergent and discriminant validity were established through CFA as described in Section 5.2. All AVE values exceeded 0.50 and all HTMT values were below 0.85, supporting both convergent and discriminant validity.

**Common Method Bias**: Given that predictors and outcomes were collected from the same respondents at a single time point, common method variance (CMV) is a potential concern. Procedural remedies included scale format variation, temporal separation between predictor and outcome items, and inclusion of a marker variable (Lindell & Whitney, 2001). Statistical post-hoc assessment via Harman's single factor test accounted for less than 24% of variance, and a confirmatory latent common method factor approach (Podsakoff et al., 2003) did not substantially alter structural path estimates (Δβ < .04 across all paths).

**Triangulation**: Cross-method triangulation between survey findings and interview accounts was used to evaluate the convergent validity of key constructs. Where quantitative and qualitative findings diverged, divergence is reported transparently and interpreted as evidence of construct complexity rather than methodological artifact.

## 8. Limitations and Bias

**Self-Selection Bias**: Participation in the Qualtrics panel is non-random; panelists may differ systematically from the target population in technology familiarity, survey-taking propensity, and employment characteristics. While stratification and quota enforcement mitigate some distributional concerns, panel-based samples remain vulnerable to coverage error.

**Social Desirability**: Retrospective self-report measures — particularly self-assessed productivity and organizational culture perceptions — are susceptible to social desirability responding. While anonymity was emphasized in survey instructions, this concern cannot be fully eliminated in self-report designs.

**Cross-Sectional Limitations**: The Phase 1 survey captures a single temporal snapshot, precluding causal inferences about directionality. Remote work intensity and outcomes may both be influenced by unmeasured third variables (e.g., job autonomy, occupational prestige). Panel-based longitudinal follow-up is planned for a subsequent study phase.

**Generalizability**: The U.S.-based sample limits geographic generalizability. Knowledge worker definitions exclude large segments of the workforce, particularly those in manual, healthcare-frontline, or customer-facing roles for whom remote work is structurally unavailable. Findings should not be extended to these populations without further research.

**Interviewer Effects**: Semi-structured interview data are co-constructed by researcher and participant. The primary investigator's professional background in organizational development may have introduced subtle framing effects despite structured probe standardization.

## 9. Ethical Considerations

### 9.1 IRB Approval

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Westlake University (Protocol #IRB-2024-1183-SBE, approved September 18, 2024). The study was classified as Exempt Category 2 for the survey phase and Expedited Review Category 6 for the interview phase, given the absence of sensitive identifiers and the minimal risk profile of the research procedures.

### 9.2 Informed Consent

All participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. For the survey, consent was embedded as a required acknowledgment page before the instrument. For interviews, written consent was obtained electronically via Qualtrics 48 hours before the scheduled session, and verbal consent was re-confirmed at the start of each recording.

Participants were informed of: the study's purpose; voluntary participation and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty; data confidentiality and anonymization procedures; data retention policies (de-identified data retained for 7 years per institutional policy); and contact information for the IRB and the Principal Investigator.

### 9.3 Data Security and Anonymization

All survey response data were stored on password-protected Qualtrics servers with encryption at rest and in transit. Personally identifiable information (name, email address) was stored in a separate, access-controlled table linked to response data by a random participant ID only. Interview recordings and transcripts were stored on encrypted institutional research storage. All identifiers were removed from transcripts before NVivo import; participants are referred to by pseudonyms assigned at transcription.

De-identified data and analysis code will be deposited in the OSF repository (https://osf.io) upon manuscript acceptance, consistent with open science practices and the journal's data sharing policy.
