# Kyoto: A Practical Travel Guide

*Ancient capital of Japan. Home to over 1,600 Buddhist temples, 400 Shinto shrines, and some of the finest kaiseki cuisine in the world. Kyoto rewards slow travel — this is not a city to check off in a day trip.*

---

## Getting There

### From Tokyo by Shinkansen

The fastest and most practical way to reach Kyoto from Tokyo is the Tokaido Shinkansen (bullet train), operated by JR Central. The **Nozomi** (fastest, non-JR Pass eligible) covers the 513km distance in approximately **2 hours 15 minutes**. The **Hikari** takes roughly 2 hours 40 minutes and is covered by the JR Pass.

| Train Type | Duration | Standard Fare (Unreserved) | JR Pass Eligible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nozomi | ~2h 15m | ¥13,320 | No |
| Hikari | ~2h 40m | ¥13,320 | Yes |
| Kodama | ~3h 45m | ¥13,320 | Yes |

**JR Pass note:** The 7-day JR Pass (purchased abroad) costs approximately ¥50,000. If your trip includes Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and Hiroshima, the pass typically pays for itself. Purchase before arriving in Japan — onsite purchase is available but at a significant premium.

Trains depart Tokyo Station from Track 14-19 (Tokaido/Sanyo Shinkansen platforms). Arrive at least 10 minutes before departure; the Shinkansen departs within 10 seconds of its scheduled time.

### From Osaka

Kyoto is 75km from Osaka. The fastest option is the **JR Special Rapid Service** from Osaka Station to Kyoto Station: 28 minutes, ¥560. Alternatively, the Hankyu Kyoto Line runs from Umeda to Kawaramachi Station in central Gion in approximately 43 minutes for ¥410.

### By Air

Kansai International Airport (KIX) serves Osaka/Kyoto. From KIX to Kyoto: take the Haruka Limited Express directly to Kyoto Station (~75 minutes, ¥2,850 with IC card discount). Itami Airport (ITM, domestic only) connects to Osaka, from which you continue by train.

## Neighborhoods

### Higashiyama (Eastern Mountains)

The best-preserved historic district in Kyoto, Higashiyama stretches along the base of the eastern mountains from Gion to Kiyomizu-dera. The main pedestrian lane, **Ninenzaka** (2-year slope) and **Sannenzaka** (3-year slope), are lined with traditional machiya townhouses converted to shops selling ceramics, matcha confections, textiles, and lacquerware. Arrive before 9:00 AM to beat the crowds.

Higashiyama is also home to **Kodai-ji Temple**, with exquisite Momoyama-period garden design, and the atmospheric **Yasaka Shrine**, which stays open 24 hours and illuminates lanterns at night.

### Arashiyama (Western Outskirts)

Located at the western edge of Kyoto, Arashiyama (roughly 30 minutes by bus from central Kyoto) is defined by the iconic **bamboo grove**, the **Togetsukyo Bridge** over the Oi River, and temple gardens of extraordinary quality. **Tenryu-ji** (UNESCO World Heritage Site, admission ¥500) has a Zen garden designed by Muso Soseki that frames Arashiyama mountain as a borrowed landscape.

The area is best reached via the **Randen (Keifuku) Tram** from Shijo-Omiya Station (¥250), which deposits you at Arashiyama Station amid the cherry trees. The bamboo grove is a five-minute walk from the station. Go at dawn — by 10 AM it is impassable with tourists.

### Gion

Kyoto's famous geisha district occupies the east bank of the Kamo River between Shijo and Sanjo streets. The area divides into Gion Kobu (main geisha district, higher-end ochaya teahouses) and Gion Higashi. Walking **Hanamikoji Street** at dusk, you may spot a maiko (apprentice geisha) hurrying to an engagement. Photography etiquette: observe, do not pursue or block. Since 2019, photography in certain lanes is restricted; posted signs must be respected.

The **Gion Corner** cultural show (¥3,150 per person, 7:00 PM and 8:00 PM most evenings) offers 45-minute condensed performances of seven traditional arts including tea ceremony, ikebana, bugaku court dance, and koto music.

### Fushimi

Fushimi district, in southern Kyoto, is home to the **Fushimi Inari Taisha** shrine and the **Fushimi sake district**. Several breweries (kura) along the Horikawa canal offer tasting tours, including Gekkeikan (one of Japan's largest sake producers, founded 1637; museum admission ¥400 including samples) and Kizakura.

## Temples and Shrines

### Kinkaku-ji (Golden Pavilion)

**Address:** 1 Kinkakujicho, Kita-ku, Kyoto | **Admission:** ¥500 | **Hours:** 9:00 AM – 5:00 PM daily

The three-story pavilion covered in gold leaf (except the ground floor) sits on the edge of Mirror Pond and is one of the most photographed structures in Japan. The original structure was burned down by a novice monk in 1950 (an incident fictionalized in Mishima's novel); the current building is a 1955 reconstruction. Crowds are heavy from 10 AM–3 PM. The site is compact — plan 30–45 minutes.

**Tip:** Purchase tickets at the gate; they come printed as a paper ofuda charm, which is both a souvenir and the ticket. Keep it.

### Fushimi Inari Taisha

**Address:** 68 Fukakusa Yabunouchicho, Fushimi-ku, Kyoto | **Admission:** Free | **Hours:** 24 hours

The shrine complex is most famous for its approximately 10,000 vermillion torii gates (each donated by a business or individual) that form a network of hiking trails up 233-meter Inari Mountain. The full loop to the summit and back takes approximately **2–3 hours**. The lower trails (to Yotsutsuji intersection, about 1 hour) offer the iconic dense-tunnel photographs. The shrine is accessible 24 hours and is dramatically atmospheric at night and early morning.

The shrine is 5 minutes walk from Inari Station on the JR Nara Line (from Kyoto Station, 5 minutes, ¥150). Arrive before 8:00 AM to photograph the lower gates without crowds.

### Kiyomizu-dera

**Address:** 1-294 Kiyomizu, Higashiyama-ku, Kyoto | **Admission:** ¥500 | **Hours:** 6:00 AM – 6:00 PM (extended hours during autumn illumination)

Built on a wooden stage (butai) cantilevered over a hillside using no nails — 139 zelkova pillars support the main hall without metal fasteners. The platform offers panoramic views of Kyoto and the surrounding mountains. The complex also includes Otawa Waterfall, where three streams (said to grant longevity, success in studies, and fortunate love) are consumed via long-handled cups.

The climb from the Gojo-zaka or Chawanzaka bus stop is steep (about 15 minutes uphill) along shopping lanes — plan accordingly in summer heat.

### Ryoan-ji

**Address:** 13 Ryoanji Goryonoshitacho, Ukyo-ku, Kyoto | **Admission:** ¥600 | **Hours:** 8:00 AM – 5:00 PM (8:30 AM – 4:30 PM December–February)

Home to Japan's most famous karesansui (dry landscape garden) — 15 rocks arranged in white gravel across a 30m x 10m rectangle. The arrangement is deliberately designed so that no matter where you stand on the viewing veranda, you can only see 14 of the 15 rocks at once. No contemporary documentation explains the garden's intended meaning. The garden was designed in the late 15th century; the author is unknown.

## Food and Dining

### Kaiseki

Kyoto is the birthplace of kaiseki, the multi-course haute cuisine rooted in tea ceremony aesthetics. A proper kaiseki meal at a top ryotei (traditional restaurant) runs ¥20,000–¥50,000 per person at dinner. For an accessible introduction, lunch kaiseki at mid-tier restaurants ranges ¥5,000–¥12,000. Notable areas: Kinosaki, the alley restaurants off Ninenzaka, and the north end of Pontocho.

### Pontocho Alley

A narrow pedestrian lane running parallel to the Kamo River between Shijo and Sanjo streets, Pontocho is lined with restaurants ranging from high-end kaiseki to casual yakitori. In summer (May–September), many restaurants open **kawayuka** (riverside platforms) cantilevered over the water. Reservations are strongly recommended for kawayuka dining; walk-in chances are highest before 11:30 AM for lunch.

### Nishiki Market

**Address:** Nishiki Market Street, between Teramachi and Takakura, Nakagyo-ku | **Hours:** Most stalls 9:00 AM – 6:00 PM (some close Monday or Wednesday)

Known as "Kyoto's Kitchen," Nishiki is a 400-meter covered arcade containing approximately 130 vendors selling pickled vegetables (tsukemono), fresh tofu, live seafood, grilled skewers, sesame products, dried goods, and kitchen equipment. It is primarily a working food market, not a tourist attraction — though tourists have largely taken over. The best time to visit is weekday mornings. Try: yudofu (simmered tofu), fresh nama-fu (wheat gluten), and asazuke (quick-pickled cucumbers).

### Matcha Culture

Kyoto's Uji district (30 minutes south by JR Nara Line) is Japan's premier matcha-producing region. In Kyoto proper, matcha cafés and patisseries are concentrated in Higashiyama and Gion. Notable: **Nakamura Tokichi** (Uji; matcha parfaits ¥1,200–¥1,800), **Ippodo Tea** (Teramachi; upstairs tea room, matcha and wagashi set ¥1,000), and numerous seasonal matcha soft-serve stalls along Sannenzaka (¥450–¥700).

## Transportation Within Kyoto

### City Buses

Kyoto City Bus operates frequent service covering most tourist sites. The **Day Pass (一日乗車券)** costs **¥700** and is valid on all city bus routes for unlimited rides. It is sold at the bus information center at Kyoto Station's east exit and at most convenience stores. Bus route maps are available in English at the station.

**Key routes:**
- Route 100 (Higashiyama Shuttle): Kyoto Station → Gion → Kiyomizu → Nanzen-ji
- Route 205 (Loop): Kyoto Station → Nijo Castle → Kinkaku-ji → Kitaoji → Heian Shrine
- Route 11: Kyoto Station → Kinkaku-ji (direct, faster than 205)

IC cards (Suica, ICOCA, PiTaPa) are accepted on all buses; individual fares are ¥230 within the city center.

### Cycling

Kyoto is exceptionally cycle-friendly — the city is largely flat (except the mountain periphery) and has dedicated cycling lanes on major streets. Bicycle rental is widely available near Kyoto Station and in Gion. Rates: ¥1,000–¥1,500/day for standard bikes, ¥1,500–¥2,500/day for electric-assist. **Strongly recommended for Fushimi and Arashiyama**, where buses are slow and crowded.

### Subway

Kyoto Subway operates two lines: the Karasuma Line (north-south) and the Tozai Line (east-west). The subway is the fastest way to reach Nijo Castle, Karasuma Oike, and Daigo-ji. Day pass: ¥800 (subway only) or ¥1,100 (combined subway + bus).

## Seasonal Tips

### Spring: Cherry Blossom Season

Peak cherry blossom (sakura) in Kyoto typically falls in **late March to early April** (usually March 25 – April 10, varying year to year). The Philosopher's Path (Tetsugaku-no-Michi), a 2km stone canal walkway lined with 450 cherry trees, is the quintessential hanami spot. Book accommodations 3–6 months in advance; prices double during peak bloom. Maruyama Park is open 24 hours and hosts raucous hanami parties under a famous weeping cherry tree.

### Autumn: Maple Foliage

Koyo (autumn leaf season) typically peaks in **mid-to-late November** (usually November 15–30). The mountain temples — Tofuku-ji, Eikan-do, and Kibune — turn crimson and gold. Many temples extend evening hours during koyo with illuminated garden events (admission charged separately, typically ¥600–¥1,000). Book even earlier than spring: November is frequently Kyoto's busiest month.

### Summer and Winter

Summer (July–August) is hot (35°C+) and humid; crowds thin slightly as conditions are challenging. The **Gion Matsuri festival** (July; main procession July 17) is one of Japan's most important festivals. Yoiyama (eve of the procession, July 16) sees Gion closed to traffic for street food and traditional floats. Winter offers the clearest skies, occasional snow at temples, and the lowest accommodation prices.

### Money-Saving Tips

- The **Kansai Thru Pass** (2-day ¥4,480 / 3-day ¥5,600) covers subway, private railways (Hankyu, Kintetsu), and some buses — useful if visiting Osaka and Nara.
- Most temple admission fees are ¥500–¥600; budget ¥3,000–¥4,000/day for dedicated temple touring.
- Convenience store meals (onigiri, sandwiches, hot foods) are excellent and cost ¥400–¥800 for a full meal. Do not be embarrassed to eat at 7-Eleven.
- Tap water is safe throughout Japan. Carry a reusable bottle.

---

*Kyoto uses Japan Standard Time (JST, UTC+9), year-round. Japan does not observe daylight saving time. English signage at major temples is generally excellent; navigation apps (Google Maps, Japan Offline Maps) work well with transit data.*
